# Cold call kit — everything you say, written out

`SALES_PLAYBOOK.md` is the strategy. This is the **words**: openers, voicemails,
emails, and the answers to what people actually say back. Copy them, change the
names, use them. Nothing here needs adapting before a call.

The one rule that outranks every script: **never claim something the product
does not do.** Section 8 of the playbook is the list. A buyer who catches one
exaggeration stops believing all of it, and honesty is this product's whole
edge.

---

## 1. Before you dial (five minutes, not fifty)

1. What kind of place is it — residential, adult day, in-home, library, adult ed?
2. Who runs activities or programs? Name and title.
3. One specific thing about them: a class on their calendar, a recent review, a
   grant they announced. **One.** You will use it in the first sentence.
4. Have the site open on Unit 1. If they say "show me now", you have 90 seconds
   to be useful.

Log after every call, win or lose: who, what they said about their learners,
their objection in *their* words, next step and date. The objection log is how
this kit gets better.

---

## 2. The gatekeeper

You will get a receptionist first. They are not an obstacle; they are the person
who knows who owns the problem.

> "Hi — I'm hoping you can point me in the right direction. Who organizes
> activities or classes for your residents? … And are they usually easiest in
> the morning or afternoon?"

If asked what it's about, be plain — vagueness gets you screened:

> "It's a computer-skills course for older beginners. I'd rather ask them two
> questions than pitch you, honestly."

If they offer to take a message, take the name anyway and ask for the direct
line or the email format.

---

## 3. The opener (first fifteen seconds)

Say your name, why *them*, and ask permission. Then stop talking.

**Residential / adult day:**
> "Hi [name], this is [you]. I saw your activities calendar runs something every
> weekday — that's why I called rather than emailed. I've built a computer-skills
> course for complete beginners, the kind where somebody learns to click before
> they learn email. Is now a bad time for two questions?"

**Library / adult ed:**
> "Hi [name], this is [you]. You run digital-literacy help at the desk, and I'm
> guessing you can't staff one-to-one for everyone who asks. I've built
> something for exactly that gap. Have you got ninety seconds?"

**In-home care:**
> "Hi [name], this is [you]. Families ask you two things — is my mother safe, and
> can I see her more. I've built a course that teaches video calls and scam
> recognition to people who have never used a computer. Can I ask you two
> questions about your clients?"

If they say now is bad: **"Totally fair — is Thursday morning better, or should
I send one paragraph by email?"** Then honor exactly what they pick.

---

## 4. The two questions that do the selling

Do not pitch. Ask, then shut up and take notes.

1. **"How do you handle it today when a resident wants to learn the computer —
   is that a class, a volunteer, or does it mostly not happen?"**
2. **"What do they actually ask for? Email, video calls with the family, or is
   it usually after something went wrong — a scam call, a fake email?"**

Whatever they name, name it back and stop:

> "That's Unit 5 — video calls and messaging. It's the unit families ask for."
> "That's Unit 10 — a practice inbox where clicking the scam costs nothing."

Then one qualifying question, asked lightly:

> "If this worked, who besides you would need to say yes?"

---

## 5. The twenty-second version (only when asked "what is it?")

> "It's a computer course where the learner does everything themselves inside a
> practice computer they cannot break — clicking, typing, email, video calls,
> spotting scams. Nothing to install, it's a website. And at the end of every
> unit they do the real thing on their own machine and the page checks they
> actually did it. No quizzes anywhere; quizzes test remembering, and this is
> about being able to."

If they want one more line, use the proof, once:

> "**163 of the 169 activities** are machine-tested end to end, every
> time we change anything. I'd rather show you than describe it."

---

## 6. The ask

Never end without a named next step and a date. Preferred order:

1. **Demo:** "Fifteen minutes on a screen share — I'll make the mistakes a
   beginner makes so you can see what happens when it goes wrong. Tuesday or
   Thursday?"
2. **Pilot direct** (if they are hot): "Six residents, Unit 1, one hour a week
   for four weeks. Costs you a room and an hour. Can I send the checklist?"
3. **Link + a date to talk:** "I'll send one link — the first lesson takes
   ninety seconds. Can I call you Friday to hear what you thought?"

---

## 7. Voicemail (twenty seconds, never longer)

**First attempt:**
> "Hi [name], [you] here, [number]. I've built a computer-skills course for
> older beginners — the kind that starts with learning to click. I'd like to ask
> you two questions about your residents, not pitch you. [number] again. Thanks."

**Second attempt, a week later:**
> "Hi [name], [you] again, [number]. Last one from me — I'll send an email
> instead so you can look when it suits. If computer help for residents isn't
> something you're working on, just tell me and I'll stop. Thanks."

Two voicemails, then email only. Chasing further costs you the referral.

---

## 8. Emails (subject lines matter more than bodies)

**Cold, after no answer.** Subject: *Computer classes for your residents*
> Hi [name],
>
> I build a computer-skills course for complete beginners — people who have
> never used a mouse. It runs in a browser, there's nothing to install, and the
> learner practices inside a computer they can't break.
>
> Two things make it different from the free video sites: every lesson is
> hands-on with the next step highlighted, and at the end of each unit the
> learner does the real thing on their own machine and the page checks it.
>
> Worth fifteen minutes? The first lesson takes ninety seconds to try:
> [link]
>
> [you], [number]

**After a demo.** Subject: *The [thing they reacted to] we looked at*
> Hi [name],
>
> Thanks for the time. You asked about [their question] — [the honest answer,
> including "not yet" if that is the answer].
>
> The thing you reacted to was [the specific moment]. Here's the direct link:
> [link]
>
> I'd like to propose six residents through Unit 1, an hour a week for four
> weeks. I'll bring the machine checklist. Does [date] work?
>
> [you]

**Breakup, after three no-replies.** Subject: *Closing the loop*
> Hi [name],
>
> I haven't heard back, which usually means this isn't a priority right now —
> completely fine. I'll stop emailing.
>
> If it comes up later, the course is at [link] and I'm at [number].
>
> [you]

This one gets replies more often than any other. Mean it anyway.

**LinkedIn note (300 characters):**
> Hi [name] — I build a computer-skills course for older beginners (clicking and
> typing up to email and spotting scams). Not selling here; I'd value two
> minutes on what your residents actually ask for.

## 9. What they say, and what you say back

Rapid reference. The long versions are in `SALES_PLAYBOOK.md §5` and `§5b`.

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Send me some information." | "Happy to — one link or a one-pager?" Then: "So I send the right one, are you thinking group sessions or one-to-one?" *(Gets you the discovery you lost.)* |
| "We already do this." | "Good — most places that say that mean a volunteer once a month. How's it going when they're away?" |
| "AARP does this for free." | "They do, and they're good — if the person can make a Tuesday at 2pm and get one of the twelve seats. How many of yours does that describe?" |
| "No budget." | "You don't need one — it's free. No paid tier, no per-seat fee, nothing to sign. The only cost is a room and an hour of somebody's time." |
| "Our residents aren't interested." | "Some aren't. The ones who are usually want one specific thing — seeing grandchildren, or not being caught by another scam call. Is either of those live for you?" |
| "They can't learn this." | "That's the assumption the whole thing is built against. Lesson one is learning to click, nothing is timed, and nothing can be broken. Pick the six you think are most likely and prove me wrong." |
| "Talk to my boss." | "Glad to — would you introduce me, or should I use your name?" *(Always ask; a forwarded intro beats a cold call.)* |
| "Call me next quarter." | "I'll do that. So I'm not wasting your time — what would need to be different by then?" |
| "Is it secure? Private?" | "There's nothing to secure — no account, no email, no cookies, no analytics, and the site doesn't contact any other company. The only thing kept is a list of finished lessons in their own browser. Open the developer tools and check; the cookie jar is empty." |
| "Can we see who's progressing?" | "No, and that's deliberate. Watching people's progress means collecting data about them, and this course collects nothing at all — no account, no cookies, no analytics. What you get instead is the certificate they print at the end of a unit, and what you can see in the room." |

## 10. Call-block routine

- Twenty dials in one sitting, twice a week. Fewer, and you never get fluent.
- Best hours for care and library staff: 9:30–11:30, and 2:00–4:00. Avoid
  mealtimes and shift change.
- After each call write **one line** in their words. Do it before the next dial.
- Every fifth call, re-read §3 aloud. Openers rot fastest.
- Any objection you could not answer goes into `SALES_PLAYBOOK.md §5` the same
  day, with the answer you wish you had given.

## 11. What never to say

- Any number you have not personally sourced this week, especially about elder
  fraud.
- "Studies show", "everyone is using", "the leading platform". All unearned.
- Anything on the roadmap said as though it exists: Spanish, a third-party WCAG
  audit, certificate verification codes, a phone/tablet guidance page.
- **Accounts, sign-in, or progress that follows a learner between machines.**
  None of it exists — it was removed on 2026-07-28 and is not coming back.
  Progress lives in one browser on one machine. Say that plainly; it is the
  other side of the privacy claim that wins these calls, not a gap to apologize
  for.
- A future price. The course is free; do not hint at a paid tier that does not
  exist, and do not promise it will always be free in writing.
- Any per-learner reporting. There is no dashboard and no plan for one, because
  the product deliberately collects nothing.
